01 / Executive recommendation

Build a hybrid network.
Earn the right to scale it.

Uber should strengthen the economics of human-driven EVs, run a bounded FSD assistance pilot, and integrate qualified third-party Level 4 fleets in stages. It should optimize for safe, reliable transportation and durable marketplace value.

Tesla’s consumer FSD (Supervised) still requires an attentive driver; it cannot be treated as driver-out supply or used to justify eliminating driver compensation. Level 4 systems perform the driving task without a human driver within their supported operating domain. This analysis concerns consumer FSD assistance and separately qualified Level 4 service, regardless of manufacturer. [2] [3]

The case’s strategic lesson is that adoption depends on economics, infrastructure, product design, and stakeholder alignment reinforcing one another. Uber should apply that lesson to autonomy while recognizing a harder distributional problem: the same AV that improves rider availability may take trips from an incumbent driver. [1, pp. 10–18]

The decision in one sentence

Fund the next increment of supply only when it improves customer value and sustainable economics, with explicit safety and driver-outcome constraints.

Scope: strategic recommendations for the case assignment, supplemented by research available by November 3, 2026. Proposed experiments, gates, and illustrative scenarios are not reported Uber results.

02 / Evidence and its limits

Promising technology.
Uneven marketplace evidence.

+50%

Electrification can accelerate through AV supply.

In the case, Uber executives report a 50% relative increase in Austin’s EV trip share after the Waymo launch, and describe AVs potentially operating 20 hours daily. This is not a 50-percentage-point increase or proof of profitability. [1, pp. 17–18]

PM implication: measure occupied miles, charging capacity, and incremental demand before interpreting operating hours as value.

56.7M

Safety evidence must match the deployment.

Kusano and colleagues’ 2025 study examined 56.7 million Waymo rider-only miles and found significantly lower injury-reported, airbag-deployment, and suspected serious-injury-or-worse crash rates against matched human benchmarks. [5]

Limit: this is retrospective, provider-associated research in specific operating conditions. It does not establish equivalent performance for Tesla FSD, another AV provider, or every new city.

−5.32%

Driver productivity can deteriorate as AVs expand.

Gridwise’s January 2026 release reports a 5.32% decline in human-driver trips per hour in five AV-active markets versus 2.56% nationally, using Q1 2024–Q4 2025 data. Three of the five markets had declining gross hourly earnings. [6]

Limit: observational city comparisons do not isolate AV causality; gross pay also differs from net earnings. Uber’s expectation of minimal driver impact is a company position, not a substitute for cohort-level evaluation. [4]

EV

Infrastructure and price remain product constraints.

The case reports that riders resisted a green price premium but sometimes accepted longer waits. It also identifies charging access as a leading adoption barrier. [1, pp. 5–6, 17]

PM implication: test AV willingness to pay independently; an EV preference is not evidence of an AV preference. Build charging and pickup reliability into the launch plan.

Choose a portfolio, with a clear priority.

A proprietary driving investment would require evidence that control creates more value than partnerships after accounting for cost, time, and risk.

04 / Product design and incentives

Solve the user’s problem at every step.

Human-driver product

Predictable take-home earnings

Drivers need viable vehicle costs, fair access to requests, and control over how they work. Offer charging discounts, transparent total-cost estimates, battery-aware dispatch, and targeted EV adoption support across brands.

Rider product

A dependable trip, end to end

Riders need an understandable price, accurate pickup, a comfortable ride, and fast help when something fails. Automation is valuable only if the whole journey works.

FSD: reward learning and outcomes, not engagement.

  1. Recruit existing eligible Tesla drivers.

    Offer an opt-in, capped subscription trial and paid training. Confirm permitted commercial use, insurance arrangements, compatible hardware, and software versions before enrollment. Do not recruit through promises of future driverless income.

  2. Preserve supervision and driver control.

    Never pay per FSD-engaged mile, for longer shifts, or for avoiding takeovers. Pay equally for study participation and reporting, including negative feedback. Drivers retain discretion to disengage; a necessary takeover is not a performance failure.

  3. Subsidize only incremental value.

    Assess workload, retention, service quality, and attributable costs against comparable Tesla operation without the subscription offer. Do not confuse existing EV savings with FSD benefits. Dispatch priority based only on FSD ownership would shift income without proving value.

Level 4: integrate the service, not just the vehicle.

Before the tripClear choiceShow AV eligibility, fare, pickup location, and accessibility capabilities. Allow riders to decline an AV without a penalty. Test willingness to pay separately from novelty.
During the tripReliable controlProvide vehicle identification, unlocking, trip progress, and a visible help channel. Distinguish remote support from an onboard human driver.

Humans can cover demand peaks and trips outside the AV operating domain, but that complementarity is conditional. If AV fleets capture profitable baseline demand, human drivers may leave, weakening the very coverage Uber needs. More trips per AV can therefore reduce resilience across the whole network.

Transition policy

Provide expansion notice, driver consultation, and targeted temporary support for demonstrated losses. Offer paid fleet-service pathways where real jobs exist, without claiming they can replace all displaced income. Do not encourage drivers to finance AV ownership unless access, operating costs, utilization, and residual-value risks are transparent.

06 / Economics and incrementality

More AV trips do not necessarily mean more value.

Uber should evaluate platform and partner economics separately. Removing the onboard driver removes one cost while increasing the importance of fleet capital and operating utilization. Human-driver earnings are neither automatically an AV cost saving for Uber nor an interchangeable measure of partner profit.

Three economic views, one investment decision

Uber contribution = net platform revenue − incentives − support − insurance and other attributable costs

Fleet profit = partner receipts − depreciation/lease − charging − maintenance − remote support − insurance − depot costs

Driver net hourly earnings = (payouts + tips + incentives − attributable vehicle and operating costs) ÷ total working time

Allocate costs once to the responsible party; avoid double counting insurance or support. Show driver earnings with and without temporary incentives. Include waiting, repositioning, and attributable charging time. Estimate returns under downside utilization and electricity-cost scenarios.

For a simplified decision model, let A be AV trips, i the share that creates incremental Uber trips, and mA and mH Uber’s contribution per AV and human-driven trip. Then the approximate contribution change is A × mA − A × (1 − i) × mH − rollout and transition costs. This makes cannibalization explicit; it still omits longer-term retention, pricing, and supply responses.

07 / Measurement architecture

Measure value creation.
Put constraints around the tradeoffs.

Primary product outcomeIncremental completed trips that meet the promised service standard, alongside sustained rider repeat usage.
Economic decision measureIncremental contribution after cannibalization, incentives, support, and transition costs; require sustainable partner returns.

Safety, driver welfare, and accessibility are independent constraints. They should not disappear inside a weighted average score.

Report outcomes by provider, software version, service area, time of day, driver tenure, and vehicle ownership or rental status. Retain drivers who stop working in cohort analyses. For exited drivers, report weekly income and exits separately rather than inventing an hourly wage when hours are zero.

The CPUC’s AV reporting framework already distinguishes pickup mileage, dwell time, occupancy, accessible requests, and unfulfilled service. These are useful operational categories, although applicable requirements must be assessed for each launch jurisdiction. [7]

Threshold discipline: pre-register economically meaningful guardrails and sample-size requirements from baseline variability, legal requirements, and stakeholder commitments. The case does not support invented universal numerical safety or earnings thresholds.

08 / Learning agenda

Test the mechanism, not just the headline.

Experiment A · FSD

Does assistance create incremental value?

Randomize the subscription offer among consenting, eligible drivers who do not already subscribe; give both groups comparable study compensation. Use an intent-to-treat analysis, record uptake and software changes, and retain non-users and exits in the analysis.

Measure workload, net costs, retention, and service outcomes. Set sample size and duration from baseline variance. Treat safety signals as monitoring inputs; a small incentive trial cannot establish rare-crash superiority.

Experiment B · Level 4

Does new supply grow healthy demand?

Use staged service-area rollout with matched controls, pre-trend checks, and spillover monitoring. For reversible matching or pricing policies, consider geographic/time switchbacks with suitable washout periods and cluster-aware inference.

Do not treat individual riders as independent when they compete for shared vehicles. DoorDash’s published switchback methods explain why region/time assignment can address marketplace interference. [8]

Test pricing and supply separately. First establish reliable service at a clearly stated price. Then vary incentives or price within appropriate constraints to estimate elasticity and repeat demand. Introducing heavy discounts, new supply, and matching changes simultaneously makes results hard to interpret.

Triangulate quantitative and qualitative evidence. Interview drivers in the most exposed cohorts, riders who decline or abandon AV trips, accessibility users, and support agents handling failures. Investigate unexpected results before averaging them away. Review results after launch promotions end.

09 / Execution and strategic leadership

A phased roadmap with real stop conditions.

Illustrative sequencing; readiness and statistical evidence determine timing. These are proposed phases, not a forecast of regulatory or technical approval.

  1. Phase 1
    Define

    Research, contracts, and readiness

    Rider and driver PMs define needs and baselines. Safety and policy validate the launch domain and permissions. Finance agrees cost allocation; operations verifies charging and recovery. Establish a driver advisory group and explicit pause authority.

  2. Phase 2
    Pilot

    Launch bounded, separately measured tests

    Run the opt-in FSD offer and a qualified Level 4 pilot with a capacity cap. Ship the full rider support journey, instrumentation, and human fallback. Publish driver-facing information about service areas and expected changes.

  3. Phase 3
    Evaluate

    Expand, revise, or stop

    Review safety evidence, customer repeat behavior, incremental contribution, partner viability, and affected driver cohorts. Expand only with sufficient evidence. Insufficient data is a reason to continue learning; a breached guardrail is a reason to intervene.

  4. Phase 4
    Scale

    Institutionalize accountability

    A city-level cross-functional team owns rollout, with independent safety escalation. Review operations weekly, investment decisions quarterly, and publish understandable city-level outcomes. Diversify partners when justified by reliability and bargaining risks.

Strategic leadership requires aligning product, policy, operations, and finance around the same outcomes. The case’s Go Get Zero mechanism illustrates the value of public commitments and recurring accountability; for autonomy, credible reporting must include constraints and driver effects as well as adoption. [1, pp. 15–18]

Uber should lead the transition by making the marketplace better for riders and economically durable for its supply partners—while explicitly managing, measuring, and mitigating the losses borne by human drivers.
